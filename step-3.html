Step 3 · Shared issue 03

How did exploration become a search?

We moved beyond isolated guesses in different ways: I searched a bounded range and checked the remaining sizes with a lower bound, Angela searched a local interval, and Manauri described an efficient way to update page-count changes.

My approach

I tested every integer page size from 1 through 100,000, compared exact integer totals, and kept ties. Because that finite search alone left larger sizes untested, I then derived a lower bound of 172,260,831 bits for every size above 100,000. It exceeds my best tested total, 101,545,439 bits, so the remaining sizes cannot win under the problem’s rules.

Angela Padilla

Angela searched every size from 32,000 through 40,000 and found 32,768 best within that interval. This is stronger than sampling a few nearby guesses, but does not by itself compare sizes below 32,000 or above 40,000.

Manauri Rodriguez

Manauri stored requests by day, tracked the largest request, and described using quotient breakpoints to record changes in total page count in a changes vector. His approach helped me notice that several consecutive page sizes can require the same number of pages. Tracking where that count changes offers a way to reduce repeated calculations during the search.

Understanding Manauri’s approach with a small example

To understand the idea in Manauri’s explanation, I can follow what happens to one 23-bit request as the page size increases.

For this request, the page count is ceil(23 / page_size). At page sizes 8, 9, 10, and 11, it remains 3 pages; at size 12, it drops to 2. With positive integer inputs in a language that truncates integer division, the ceiling can be written (request + page_size - 1) / page_size. The parentheses ensure the addition happens before division.

Connecting his approach to my calculation.

For one request, the penalty is (page size + 64) × pages − request. Adding across requests gives total penalty = (page size + 64) × total allocated pages − total useful bits. This shows why tracking total page count can help calculate the score. I recalculated each request’s contribution for every candidate page size, while Manauri described updating the page count where it changes.

Reflection. Comparing our searches helped me separate efficient scoring from search coverage. Manauri’s quotient grouping made me notice how page-count updates could reduce repeated work, while Angela’s local search showed what an exhaustive interval can establish. My own lower bound extended my 1–100,000 search to a global conclusion for this workload. In future searches, I’ll state both what I covered and why it is safe to stop.

Step 3 · Shared issue 06

What does the evidence let us conclude?

Comparing our search ranges helped me separate the best result within a tested interval from a conclusion about every permitted size.

My approach

In Step 2, I found a unique minimum at 32,768 among sizes 1–100,000, with a total of 101,545,439 bits. My lower bound for every size above 100,000 is 172,260,831 bits, larger than that total. Together, the search and bound support 32,768 as the global optimum for these workloads under the problem’s rules.

Angela Padilla

Angela found 32,768 best within the 32,000–40,000 interval she searched. Her result supports mine, but it doesn’t independently rule out sizes outside that interval.

Manauri Rodriguez

Manauri described searching page sizes through the largest request and finding a unique minimum. My result comes from enumerating 1–100,000 and showing that every larger size has a lower bound above my best total. Angela’s search covers 32,000–40,000. Those ranges and stopping arguments define what each result covers.

Why the largest request is a finite bound. Let M be the largest positive request. For any page size p ≥ M, every program fits in one page. The total then equals N(p + 64) − S, where N is the number of requests and S is their fixed useful-bit sum. Increasing p by one adds N penalty bits, so no size above M can improve on size M. This is a mathematical search-bound argument; it is separate from verifying that Manauri’s code implemented it correctly.

Reflection. Angela’s result is best within her 32,000–40,000 interval, while my search and lower bound support a global result for this workload. Manauri described a maximum-request stopping point. Seeing those different scopes together taught me to pair an optimum with a clear account of what was searched and what justifies the stopping point.

Step 3 · Feedback and response

Feedback we exchanged

Angela’s feedback helped me recognize the value of building my calculation in stages. Comparing my direct search with Manauri’s change-based approach also helped me see how a straightforward calculation and an optimized one can serve different purposes.

What Angela noticed about my approach

Angela pointed out that I started by checking one program, then expanded to one day’s file and finally all 32 days. Building the program this way let me check each new part against a calculation I already understood.

She also noticed that we both moved from manual trials to a search loop and reached 32,768 bits. Her comparison helped me recognize that trying individual sizes was useful for exploring, while the loop checked possibilities our guesses could miss.

What Manauri noticed about my approach

Manauri highlighted the value of having a direct calculation that is easy to trace. My program applies the same penalty formula to every request at each candidate size, so there is a straightforward route from the input data to the total. That makes the method useful as a reference when checking a more complex search.

His change-based approach gave us a useful contrast: my search repeats the page calculation for each candidate, while his approach tracks where page counts change. The methods can complement each other—a direct calculation provides a clear reference, and grouping changes offers a way to reduce repeated work.

How I responded

I kept my staged, direct approach because I could explain each part and connect the full search to my hand calculation. Angela’s feedback helped me see that those stages were useful checkpoints, rather than just steps toward a larger program.

Manauri’s approach helped me ask which calculations my program repeats unnecessarily. I did not replace my search with a grouped algorithm; instead, I took away a way to approach future optimizations: keep a simple reference calculation and compare a more efficient method against it on small cases and at boundaries where its behavior changes.

The exchange also included feedback in the other direction. I suggested that Angela include each day’s costs at the winning size and encouraged her average-based starting experiment. For Manauri’s explanation, we discussed the value of a small worked example showing when a page-count change occurs. These suggestions focused on making the reasoning easier for another person to follow.
